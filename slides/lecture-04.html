Class 3 Recap · Marginal Revenue

Marginal Revenue & Point Elasticity

Marginal revenue (MR) is the extra revenue from selling one more unit. A single-price seller facing downward-sloping demand must lower the price on every unit — so MR falls below the price:

\[ MR(Q) = P(Q)\left(1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right) \]
  • Because demand slopes down, \( \varepsilon < 0 \), so \( \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right) < 1 \) and \( MR < P \) at positive output under these assumptions. A price taker instead has MR = P.
  • Elastic demand (\( |\varepsilon| > 1 \)) \( \Rightarrow MR > 0 \): selling more still raises revenue.
  • Inelastic demand (\( |\varepsilon| < 1 \)) \( \Rightarrow MR < 0 \): selling more lowers revenue.

Class 3 Recap · Marginal Revenue

Marginal Revenue from Linear Demand

Linear inverse demand
\[ P = a - bQ \] \[ R(Q) = (a - bQ)Q = aQ - bQ^2 \] \[ MR(Q) = a - 2bQ \]
Rule of thumb
Same intercept \( a \), but twice the slope — so MR hits the quantity axis at half the demand curve's distance.

Class 3 Recap · Monopoly Pricing

Solving for the Monopolist's Price

A monopolist faces the whole market demand and chooses quantity first, then price, using the rule \( MR = MC \):

  1. Set up: from demand, get inverse demand \( P = a - bQ \) and \( MR = a - 2bQ \).
  2. Quantity: set \( MR = MC \) and solve for \( Q^* \).
  3. Price: read it off the demand curve, \( P^* = P(Q^*) = a - bQ^* \).
  4. Profit: \( \Pi = P^* Q^* - TC(Q^*) \).
Key intuition
The monopolist does not pick an arbitrary price — it is pinned down by the demand curve at \( Q^* \).

Perfect Competition · Structure

Market Characteristics

Key features of a perfectly competitive environment:

  • Large number of sellers and buyers; no individual has market power.
  • Homogeneous products: Only identical products are sold in the market, with no product differentiation.
  • No barriers to entry or exit: firms can freely enter when profits are positive and exit when they are negative.
  • Perfect information: Buyers and sellers have complete knowledge of prices and technology.
⇒ Everyone is a Price Taker
Together these imply no single buyer or seller can influence the market price — the (aggregate) market demand and supply decide the price, and every firm simply takes it as given!

Perfect Competition · Definition

Defining Perfect Competition

Definition: Perfect Competition
A form of competitive environment characterized by a large number of sellers and buyers, with a homogeneous, undifferentiated product, where every firm and every individual takes market prices as a given.

Real-world examples:

  • Markets of produce (corn, rice, grains, wheat).
  • Markets of standardized knockoffs/commodities.

Perfect Competition · Summary

Characteristics Recap

Therefore, perfectly competitive markets are characterized by:

  • Large numbers of sellers and buyers + only identical products sold in the market, with no product differentiation.
  • Market prices are taken as given by everyone.
  • Demand each firm faces is perfectly elastic.
  • Each firm’s supply is determined by its marginal costs (with some additional twists!).

Market Equilibrium · Supply

Is Marginal Cost the Supply Curve?

Firms choose quantity where \( MR = MC \). Since \( MR = P \), this means competitive firms choose quantity where:

P = MC(Q)

Does the MC curve fully represent the firm's supply decision?

  • Yes, but with nuances: The firm may choose to produce zero (shut down) if the price is too low to cover certain costs.
  • The distinction depends on whether we look at the short run or the long run.

Market Equilibrium · Profits

Profit, Loss, or Shutdown?

For an operating firm, \(\Pi=(P-ATC)q\). First find the quantity where P meets rising MC.

  • P > ATC: economic profit is positive.
  • AVC < P < ATC: operate at a loss in the short run.
  • P < min AVC: shut down; the loss equals unavoidable fixed cost.
Check
Revenue is $80, variable cost is $60, and unavoidable fixed cost is $40. Operate or shut down?
Operate: profit is −$20, versus −$40 if shut down. At P = min AVC, operation and shutdown can yield the same profit. This assumes no additional avoidable fixed operating cost.

Short-Run Equilibrium · Definition

The Short Run

At least one input is fixed. We hold the number of firms fixed while existing firms choose output.

A firm may shut down temporarily, producing zero while still owing unavoidable fixed costs.

The operating decision compares revenue with avoidable costs. Fixed costs still count when calculating profit.

Short run is defined by adjustment possibilities, not a fixed number of months. Shutdown differs from permanent exit.

Short-Run Equilibrium · Supply

The Short-Run Supply Curve

The Shutdown Rule:

  • The short-run supply curve is the section of the \( MC \) curve above the \( AVC \) minimum.
  • If \( P < \min AVC \), the firm produces zero (\( Q = 0 \)) and shuts down.
Takeaway: In short-run market equilibrium, firms will still operate even if they make economic loss, provided they cover variable costs. If they cannot cover variable costs, they shut down.

Short-Run Equilibrium · Dynamics

Free Entry and Exit

Market Adjustments:

  • Entry and exit occur as the market adjusts toward the long run; the short-run number of firms is fixed.
  • Free entry and exit are assumptions of this model. Identical products alone do not guarantee them.

Why might new firms enter, and existing firms exit, if everyone takes the market price as given?

Short-Run Equilibrium · Entry/Exit

Illustrating Entry Dynamics

Adjustment toward the long run:

  • Possible for firms in competitive markets to earn economic profit.
  • Profit encourages new firms to enter the market.
  • Market supply shifts to the right.
  • If profits are negative, everything reverses direction: firms exit the market, and market supply shifts to the left.

Long-Run Equilibrium · Definition

The Long Run

All inputs can be adjusted, and firms can enter or exit. Costs that were fixed in the short run can now be avoided or changed.

Zero economic profit
With free entry, identical firms, and unchanged input prices, entry or exit drives price to minimum average total cost.

Zero economic profit covers all economic costs, including the owner’s next-best use of time and capital.

Sunk means unrecoverable; fixed means unchanged with output over a stated horizon. These are different properties. Do not describe all fixed costs as sunk. With changing scale choices, the formal benchmark is minimum long-run average cost.

Short run
The number of firms and at least one input are fixed. Economic profit may be positive, zero, or negative. Shut down if P is below minimum AVC.
Long run
Firms can change all inputs and enter or exit. In our identical-firm model, P = minimum ATC and economic profit is zero.

Yes: if revenue covers variable costs and part of unavoidable fixed costs, operating loses less than shutting down.

Welfare · Intuition

Consumer, Producer & Social Surplus

Why care about market structure? It shapes how well-off people and firms are.

Consumer Surplus (CS)
The value buyers get above what they pay — willingness to pay minus price.
Producer Surplus (PS)
The gain sellers get above their cost — revenue minus variable cost; profit also subtracts fixed cost.
Social Welfare (Social Surplus)
The total gains from trade in a market: \[ W = CS + PS \]

Welfare · Efficiency & DWL

Efficiency & Deadweight Loss

  • Perfect competition maximizes total surplus when prices reflect all social costs and benefits — the market is efficient (no surplus left on the table).
  • Market power (e.g. monopoly) sets price above \( MC \) and restricts output.
  • The lost gains from trade are a deadweight loss (DWL) — surplus that is simply gone, transferred to no one.
This is why market structure has welfare implications for society. We study surplus, efficiency, and deadweight loss in depth in Class 5.
