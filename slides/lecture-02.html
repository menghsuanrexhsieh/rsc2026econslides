Recap · 1 of 3

Scarcity, Opportunity Cost, and the Margin

Opportunity cost
The value of the best alternative forgone. Include time and other resources, not just money.
Marginal choice
Choose an extra unit when MB exceeds MC. With whole units, equality need not occur.

For smooth, divisible choices with falling MB and rising MC, the best interior quantity satisfies \( MB = MC \). Compare endpoints too.

Recap · 2 of 3

Demand, Supply & Equilibrium

Demand — Law of Demand
How much buyers want at each price. P↑ ⇒ QD↓ (downward-sloping).
Supply — Law of Supply
How much sellers offer at each price. P↑ ⇒ QS↑ (upward-sloping).
Market equilibrium
The price P* where supply meets demand (QD = QS) — the market clears.
Excess supply
When the transaction price is above P*: QS > QD → sellers cut the price.
Excess demand
When the transaction price is below P*: QD > QS → buyers bid the price up.

Recap · 3 of 3

Movements Along vs. Shifts of Curves

Movement along a curve
Caused only by a change in the good’s own price (everything else held constant).

e.g. coffee’s price rises → slide up the demand curve, QD falls.

Shift of the whole curve
Caused by any non-price factor (income, tastes, input costs, weather, number of buyers/sellers).

e.g. a frost hits coffee farms → the entire supply curve shifts left.

But be cautious about which market we are analyzing!

Algebra in supply & demand

Shocks vs. Movements

In economic analysis, it is critical to distinguish between these effects:

Definition: Shock
Any change to market conditions is called a shock.
  • In this model, a new equilibrium follows a shift in demand or supply, holding the market rules fixed.
  • Demand or supply curve shifts occur if variables we were holding constant (tastes, income, government policies, costs of production) change.
  • Movement along the demand or supply curve is a result of only price changes.

Beyond Directions

We can ask: How can we quantify behavior at various points along the supply and demand curves?

Elasticity

What is Elasticity?

Elasticity compares percentage changes in two variables.

It measures how responsive consumers or producers are to changes in market conditions.

Elasticity · Definition

Price Elasticity Without Calculus

Price elasticity compares the percentage change in quantity demanded with the percentage change in price, holding other factors constant.

At a point on linear demand
For \( Q = a-bP \), with \(b>0\): \[ \varepsilon=-b\frac{P}{Q},\qquad |\varepsilon|=b\frac{P}{Q}. \]

Here b is the number of units quantity falls when price rises by $1. It is the magnitude of ΔQ/ΔP; the plotted P-versus-Q slope is −1/b.

For downward-sloping demand, ε is negative. We use |ε| for elastic versus inelastic.

For a curved demand function, point elasticity uses its local slope. For a finite change, specify a percentage-change convention. This slide uses linear demand so no derivative rules are required.

Elasticity · Example

Point Elasticity Varies Along the Curve

Calculation Exercise
Given the demand function: \[ Q = 10 - 0.5P \] Find |ε| at the midpoint. Then describe what happens as we approach each intercept:
  • Midpoint: \( (Q=5, P=10) \)
  • Horizontal intercept: \( (Q=10, P=0) \)
  • Vertical intercept: \( (Q=0, P=20) \)

Elasticity · Solution

Elasticity at the Midpoint and Near the Ends

For \(Q=10-0.5P\), use \(|\varepsilon|=0.5P/Q\).

Midpoint
At \(P=10, Q=5\): \(|\varepsilon|=1\).
Near the horizontal intercept
As P approaches 0, |ε| approaches 0.
Near the vertical intercept
As Q approaches 0, |ε| grows without bound. At Q = 0 the formula is undefined.

These are endpoint limits on one sloped line. Perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic demand curves are horizontal and vertical, respectively.

Elasticity · Linear demand

Elasticity Along a Linear Demand Curve

Elasticity regions on a linear curve:

  • Elastic above the midpoint: \( |\varepsilon| > 1 \)
  • Unit-elastic at the midpoint: \( |\varepsilon| = 1 \)
  • Inelastic below the midpoint: \( |\varepsilon| < 1 \)

Elasticity · Classification

Elastic or Inelastic?

  • Elastic: |ε| > 1. Quantity changes by a larger percentage than price.
  • Unit-elastic: |ε| = 1. The percentage changes have equal magnitudes.
  • Inelastic: |ε| < 1. Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price.

Demand tends to be more elastic when close substitutes exist, the purchase uses more of the budget, or buyers have more time to adjust.

Elasticity · Slopes

Compare Slopes at the Same Point

At the same price and quantity, using the same axis units, the flatter demand curve is more elastic.

Slope alone cannot compare elasticity at different points. Elasticity also depends on P/Q.

Check
A straight demand line has a constant slope. Does that mean its elasticity is constant?
No. P/Q changes along the line. The flatter-versus-steeper comparison requires common P, Q, and units; it is not a general definition of elasticity.

Answers: Point Elasticity

\[ Q = 24 - 4(3) = 12 \qquad |\varepsilon| = \left| \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \right| \times \frac{P}{Q} = 4 \times \frac{3}{12} = 1 \]

Elasticity Between Two Prices

Suppose price rises from $10 to $12 and quantity falls from 100 to 80, with other demand factors unchanged.

Use the midpoint method
\[ \varepsilon=\frac{(80-100)/90}{(12-10)/11}=-\frac{11}{9}\approx-1.22. \]

The denominators are the averages: quantity 90 and price $11. Demand is elastic over this interval.

Why use averages? Reversing the direction gives the same elasticity magnitude.

Ask students to compute the two percentage changes first: −22.22% and +18.18%. This is arc elasticity over an interval, distinct from point elasticity at a particular price. It also differs from the geometric midpoint of a demand line. This replaces the static duplicate of the preceding graph.

Other elasticity measures

Income and Cross-Price Elasticities

Income elasticity
\[ \varepsilon_I=\frac{\%\Delta Q_X}{\%\Delta I} \] Positive: normal good. Negative: inferior good.
Cross-price elasticity
\[ \varepsilon_{X,Y}=\frac{\%\Delta Q_X}{\%\Delta P_Y} \] Positive: substitutes. Negative: complements.

Keep the sign. Hold other relevant factors constant. “Inferior” describes a response to income, not product quality.

Other elasticity measures · Examples

Applying Income Elasticity

Example 1
Rex’s net income increased by 21% this year compared to last; his purchases of sparkling water rose by 17%.

What is his income elasticity of demand for sparkling water?

Is sparkling water a normal or inferior good for Rex?

Assume other relevant factors, including own price, are unchanged. The exercise supplies percentage changes, so use those as given.

Elasticity · Solution

Solution: Income Elasticity

Calculation
\[ \varepsilon_I = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta I} = \frac{17\%}{21\%} \approx 0.81 \]
Interpretation
  • Income elasticity is positive (0.81 > 0)
  • ⇒ Sparkling water is a normal good for Rex
  • A 1% increase in income leads to a ~0.81% increase in purchases

Other elasticity measures · Examples

Applying Cross-Price Elasticity

Example 2
Wicked Art Fair (WAF) and Artistic Odyssey (AO) hold fairs at the same time every year. This year, WAF raised their ticket price by 20%, and AO sees their traffic increase by 11%.

What is the cross-price elasticity of demand of (tickets for) AO?

Are WAF and AO tickets complements or substitutes?

Assume other relevant factors, including own price, are unchanged. The exercise supplies percentage changes, so use those as given.

Elasticity · Solution

Solution: Cross-Price Elasticity

Calculation
\[ \varepsilon_{AO,WAF} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_{AO}}{\%\Delta P_{WAF}} = \frac{11\%}{20\%} = 0.55 \]
Interpretation
  • Cross-price elasticity is positive (0.55 > 0)
  • ⇒ WAF and AO tickets are substitutes
  • When WAF raises prices, consumers switch to AO

Sunk Costs & Decision-Making

Decision-Making

Which Costs Matter Now?

Compare the additional revenue with the additional cost of the decision.

Sunk cost
An unrecoverable cost is the same whichever option you choose now. It should not change that choice.

A previously purchased item can still have a resale value. Keeping it gives up that value, which is an opportunity cost.

Decision-Making

Sunk Costs

Definition
Sunk costs are costs that occurred in the past and are completely irrecoverable.

Sunk costs must be ignored from the point of view of forward-looking decision-making.

Sunk Cost Fallacy
The cognitive bias where individuals/firms justify making choices based on past resource investments rather than marginal benefits/costs.

Decision-Making · Case Study 1

Headphone Reseller: Keep or Sell?

Rex paid $500 each for 1,000 headphones. That payment cannot be recovered from the original seller. Another reseller now offers $450 each.

Keeping the stock for a year requires $160 per unit in maintenance; selling now avoids it. Ignore discounting and all other costs.

Question
What minimum selling price next year would make keeping a unit at least as attractive as selling now?
Answer: $610 = $450 forgone resale revenue + $160 future maintenance. The historical $500 is sunk. If the $160 were payable either way, it would cancel instead. This replaces the original under-specified question.

Decision-Making · Case Study 2

Headphone Reseller: One More Sale

Rex has sold his existing stock. To fill one extra order, he must buy one new headphone for $500 and pay $160 to prepare that unit for sale.

His staffing, rent, utilities, and own pay do not change for this order; there is spare capacity.

Question
What is the marginal cost of filling the order?
Answer: $660. Both new payments are avoidable if he declines the order. Past inventory costs are irrelevant; future restocking is not sunk. If overtime or extra shipping were needed, include those too.

Market Structure · Introduction

Economic Profit Counts Opportunity Costs

Accounting profit
Revenue minus explicit payments, such as wages and materials.
Economic profit
Revenue minus explicit payments and implicit opportunity costs, such as the owner’s forgone earnings.

Rex earns $40,000 after paying the shop’s bills. He could earn $40,000 in another job. What is his economic profit?

Answer: zero, assuming no other implicit costs. Zero economic profit includes compensation for the owner’s next-best use of time and capital; it does not mean zero income.

Firms and Costs

Understanding Cost Functions

In economics, firms are treated as a “black box” producing goods. Because producing anything requires resources, it involves trade-offs. We capture these trade-offs in terms of cost functions.

Definition
The total cost function \( C(Q) \) (or \( TC(Q) \)) captures the sum of all economic costs for a firm producing at quantity \( Q \).

Firms and Costs

Fixed vs. Variable Costs

There are two types of production costs that we care about:

Definition: Fixed Costs (FC)
Costs that do not change with the level of quantity produced (e.g., rent, basic administration).
Definition: Variable Costs (VC)
Costs that change with the quantity produced (e.g., raw materials, hourly labor).

Firms and Costs

Total Cost and Marginal Cost

\[ C(Q)=FC+VC(Q) \]

For whole units, the cost of the next unit is C(Q + 1) − C(Q). Over a small interval, marginal cost is ΔC/ΔQ.

Example
Total cost rises from $140 for 4 units to $155 for 5 units. The fifth unit costs $15.

For smooth cost curves, we will supply the MC formula so you can focus on the economic decision.

The continuous MC formula is the derivative of total cost. It need not equal the cost of one whole extra unit. For quadratic costs, the finite difference includes an additional term. No calculus is assumed.

Firms and Costs

Profit Function with Costs

Combining revenue and cost functions, we get the complete profit function:

\[ \Pi(Q) = R(Q) - C(Q) = P(Q)Q - C(Q) \]

Profit maximization involves choosing \( Q \) to maximize this expression.

Firms and Costs

Average Cost Functions

For Q > 0, average costs divide each cost by output:

Average Total Cost (ATC)
Total cost divided by quantity: \[ ATC(Q) = \frac{C(Q)}{Q} \]
Average Fixed Cost (AFC)
Fixed cost divided by quantity: \[ AFC(Q) = \frac{FC}{Q} \]
Average Variable Cost (AVC)
Variable cost divided by quantity: \[ AVC(Q) = \frac{VC(Q)}{Q} \]

Firms and Costs · Example

Calculate Fixed, Variable, and Average Costs

\[ C(Q)=100+10Q+Q^2,\qquad MC(Q)=10+2Q\quad\text{(given)} \]

  • Identify FC and VC(Q).
  • Write ATC, AFC, and AVC for Q > 0.
  • At Q = 5, find total cost, ATC, and the given MC.

Use algebra and substitution; you do not need to differentiate.

Costs · Solution

Solution: Cost Functions

\[ FC=100,\qquad VC(Q)=10Q+Q^2 \]

\[ ATC=100/Q+10+Q,\quad AFC=100/Q,\quad AVC=10+Q \]

At Q = 5
\[ C(5)=175,\qquad ATC(5)=35,\qquad MC(5)=20. \]

The cost of the sixth whole unit is C(6) − C(5) = 196 − 175 = $21. The given smooth MC at Q = 5 is $20.

Firms and Costs · Curves

Visualizing Cost Curves

For the U-shaped average cost curves shown:

  • \( MC \) passes through the minimum points of both \( ATC \) and \( AVC \).
  • \( AFC \) drops continuously as quantity increases.
  • The gap between \( ATC \) and \( AVC \) shrinks as \( AFC \) decreases.
These are schematic curves, not the graph of the previous quadratic example, whose AVC is increasing. When MC is below an average, that average falls; above it, the average rises. At a smooth interior minimum they are equal. This is the same logic as adding a test score to an average.
