Recap · Monopoly Pricing

Monopoly Pricing: A Worked Example

The Problem
A monopolist faces market demand \( Q = 12 - 2P \) with constant marginal cost \( MC = 2 \) and no fixed costs.

Find the profit-maximizing quantity \( Q^* \), the price \( P^* \), and the profit \( \Pi \).
Step 1 · Inverse demand & marginal revenue
Solve demand for price, then use “same intercept, twice the slope”: \[ P = 6 - \frac{1}{2}Q \implies MR = 6 - Q \]
Step 2 · Quantity: set \( MR = MC \)
A monopolist picks quantity first, where marginal revenue meets marginal cost: \[ 6 - Q = 2 \implies Q^* = 4 \]

Recap · Monopoly Pricing

Reading Off the Price & the Profit

Step 3 · Price: read it off the demand curve
The monopolist does not pick an arbitrary price — it is pinned down by demand at \( Q^* \): \[ P^* = 6 - \frac{1}{2}(4) = \$4 \]
Step 4 · Profit
With no fixed costs, total cost is \( TC = MC \times Q = 2Q \): \[ \Pi = P^* Q^* - TC(Q^*) = (4)(4) - (2)(4) = \$8 \]
Takeaway
The rule is \( MR = MC \) to set the quantity, then the demand curve sets the price. Here \( (Q^*, P^*, \Pi) = (4,\ \$4,\ \$8) \).

Recap · Perfect Competition

Price Takers: Firm vs. Market Demand

In perfect competition, each firm takes the market price as given, so its marginal revenue is just that price:

\( MR = \) market price \( P \)
The demand a single firm faces is…
horizontal (perfectly elastic) at the market price — the small firm can sell its feasible output at \( P \), but nothing at a higher price.
The market demand is…
downward-sloping — the aggregate of all buyers. One tiny firm simply cannot move it.

This is the key contrast with a monopolist, who faces the whole downward-sloping market demand alone.

Recap · Perfect Competition

The Firm’s Supply Curve & Its Profits

The firm produces where \( P = MC \). Trace the price up and down the \( MC \) curve:

Above \( e_2 \) (min \( AC \)): \( P > AC \Rightarrow \) profit is positive.
At \( e_2 \): \( P = \min AC \Rightarrow \) profit is zero.
Between \( e_1 \) and \( e_2 \): \( AVC < P < AC \Rightarrow \) a loss, but keep producing (price still covers variable cost).
Below \( e_1 \) (min \( AVC \)): shut down — the price cannot even cover variable cost.

Supply curve \( = \) the \( MC \) curve above \( e_1 \) (min \( AVC \)).  (\( AC = ATC \))

Recap · Long-Run Equilibria

The Long Run: Everyone Earns Zero Profit

The benchmark result
In the long run, every firm in a perfectly competitive market earns \( \$0 \) economic profit. Why? Because there are no barriers to entry or exit.
If short-run profits are positive…
Outsiders see \( \Pi > 0 \) and enter the market. Market supply shifts right, the price falls, and profits shrink — until \( \Pi = 0 \).
If short-run profits are negative…
Firms suffering \( \Pi < 0 \) exit the market. Market supply shifts left, the price rises, and losses shrink — until \( \Pi = 0 \).
Assume identical firms, free entry, and the same technology and input prices available to entrants. Zero economic profit includes a normal return to the owner. Scarce firm-specific advantages can produce rents outside this benchmark.

Recap · Long-Run Equilibria

The Long-Run Equilibrium Price in Perfectly Competitive Markets

Entry keeps pushing the price down until the firm’s flat demand is tangent to the bottom of its \( ATC \). So the long-run price must be \( P^* = \min(ATC) \) — exactly where \( MC \) crosses the minimum of \( ATC \) (point \( e_2 \) on Group 1’s cost curves), where every firm earns zero profit.

Perfect Competition · Worked Problem

Rex's Ties Calculation

Problem Statement
Rex’s Ties makes University of Michigan Rose Bowl ties in a perfectly competitive market. Its cost functions are \[ TC(q) = q^2 + 2q + 49 \qquad MC(q) = 2q + 2 \] the current market price is $20 per tie, and market demand is \( Q_D = 268 - 8P \). Every firm in the market has these same cost functions.
(a) Find Rex’s short-run profit-maximizing quantity and its profit.
(b) How many firms are in the market in the short run?
(c) Find the long-run equilibrium price and the number of firms then.

Perfect Competition · Solution (a)

Short-Run Quantity & Profit

Step 1: Profit-maximizing quantity
A price taker produces where \( P = MR = MC \): \[ 20 = 2q + 2 \implies q^* = 9 \]
Step 2: Profit at \( q^* = 9 \)
\[ TR = 20 \cdot 9 = 180 \qquad TC = 81 + 18 + 49 = 148 \] \[ \Pi = TR - TC = 180 - 148 = \$32 \]
Since \( ATC(9) = \frac{148}{9} \approx 16.44 \) and \( P = 20 > ATC \), profit is positive — so the market is not yet in long-run equilibrium.

Perfect Competition · Solution (b)

Number of Firms in the Short Run

Step 1: Market quantity at \( P = \$20 \)
Plug the price into market demand: \[ Q_D = 268 - 8(20) = 108 \text{ ties.} \]
Step 2: Divide by each firm’s output
Every firm is identical and makes \( q^* = 9 \), so the number of firms is \[ n = \frac{Q_D}{q^*} = \frac{108}{9} = 12 \text{ firms.} \]

Perfect Competition · Solution (c)

Long Run: Price & Number of Firms

Step 1: Long-run price \( = \min(ATC) \)
Free entry drives profit to zero, so \( P^* = \min(ATC) \), found where \( ATC = MC \): \[ q + 2 + \frac{49}{q} = 2q + 2 \implies \frac{49}{q} = q \implies q = 7 \] \[ P^* = \min(ATC) = 7 + 2 + \frac{49}{7} = \$16 \]
Step 2: Number of firms in the long run
Each firm makes \( q = 7 \) (its min-ATC quantity). Market quantity at \( P = \$16 \) is \[ Q_D = 268 - 8(16) = 140 \implies n = \frac{140}{7} = 20 \text{ firms.} \]
Positive short-run profit draws firms in: the market grows from 12 to 20 firms, pushing the price from \(\$20\) down to \(\$16\), where every firm earns zero profit.

Group Work · Answers

Answers: Price Takers

\[ P = MC: 24 = 2q + 4 \implies q^* = 10 \] \[ \Pi = 24(10) - (100 + 40 + 64) = 240 - 204 = \$36 \]

Surplus · Consumer

Defining Consumer Surplus

How do we measure consumer well-being?

  • Competitive structure directly determines the market price.
  • Each consumer has their own willingness-to-pay (valuation) for a product.
  • Market price determines the welfare they receive from transacting.
Definition
Consumer surplus (CS) is the difference between total willingness to pay and total expenditure (for a given level of consumption).

Surplus · Consumer

Willingness to Pay vs. Expenditure

Willingness to Pay (WTP)
Willingness to pay is represented by the demand curve because the curve shows the maximum price a consumer is willing to pay for each incremental unit.
Total Expenditure
Total expenditure is how much it actually costs to purchase those units: \[ \text{Total Expenditure} = P^* \times Q^* \]

Let's look at this concept graphically on the next slide!

Warm-Up: Surplus from One Purchase

Example
Rex wants to buy a Nintendo Switch 2. He is willing to pay $600 for a unit. For this hypothetical purchase, the price is $500.

What is his surplus if he buys one unit of Nintendo Switch 2?
Answer
\[ CS = \text{WTP} - \text{Expenditure} = 600 - 500 = \$100 \]

Surplus · Practice

Practice: Consumer Surplus

Problem Statement
A consumer has demand function for packs of gum given by: \[ Q_D = 30 - 5P \] The market price for gum is currently $5 per pack.

Determine how many packs of gum this consumer would consume, and calculate their consumer surplus.

Surplus · Solution

Solution: Consumer Surplus

Step 1: Find Quantity Demanded
Plugging \( P = 5 \) into the demand function: \[ Q_D = 30 - 5(5) = 5 \text{ packs} \]
Step 2: Find the Demand Choke Price
Find the price where demand is exactly zero: \[ 30 - 5P = 0 \implies P_{\text{choke}} = \$6 \]
Step 3: Calculate the Triangle Area
CS is the area of the triangle below demand and above price (\(P=5\)): \[ CS = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} \times Q_D \times (P_{\text{choke}} - P) \] \[ CS = \frac{1}{2} \times 5 \times (6 - 5) = \$2.50 \]

Surplus · Producer

Producer Surplus and Profit

Producer surplus
\[ PS=TR-VC \] It is the area above marginal cost and below price, up to the quantity sold.
Economic profit
\[ \Pi=TR-VC-FC=PS-FC \]

If revenue is $100, variable cost is $60, and fixed cost is $30, what are PS and profit?

Answer: PS = $40; economic profit = $10, assuming the stated costs include all opportunity costs. Supply reflects marginal opportunity cost. Sunk fixed costs do not change the gains from an extra trade.

Surplus · Warm-Up

Warm-Up: Surplus from One Sale

Example
Nintendo is selling one unit of Nintendo Switch 2 to Rex at $500. Assume the variable cost of this extra unit is $338; this is an illustrative figure.

What is Nintendo’s surplus if they sell him one unit of Nintendo Switch 2?
Answer
\[ PS = \text{Revenue} - \text{Cost} = 500 - 338 = \$162 \]

Surplus · Practice

Practice: Producer Surplus

Problem Statement
A firm has supply function for packs of gum given by: \[ Q_S = 10 + 5P_S \] Assume MC is zero for the first 10 packs, then rises as shown by supply. Suppose the firm sells each pack of gum at the market price of $5.

Determine the producer surplus for this firm.
Supply is specified for nonnegative prices. Assume the first 10 units have zero marginal cost; beyond 10 units, MC = (Q − 10)/5. Thus total variable cost is nonnegative and surplus includes a rectangle plus a triangle. Extending the line to negative prices would be a different model and give different surplus.

Surplus · Solution

Solution: Producer Surplus

Step 1: Find Quantity Supplied
Plugging \( P = 5 \) into the supply function: \[ Q_S = 10 + 5(5) = 35 \text{ packs} \]
Step 2: Understand the Area
Supply starts at \( Q = 10 \) when \( P = 0 \). The area above the supply curve and below \( P = 5 \) is a trapezoid with height \( 5 \) (from \( P = 0 \) to \( P = 5 \)) and parallel bases of length \( 10 \) and \( 35 \).
Step 3: Calculate the Trapezoid Area
\[ PS = \frac{\text{base}_1 + \text{base}_2}{2} \times \text{height} = \frac{10 + 35}{2} \times 5 = 22.5 \times 5 = \$112.50 \]

Rectangle + triangle: PS = (10 × $5) + ½(25 × $5) = $112.50.

Surplus · Market Level

Practice: Market Surplus

Problem Statement
In the market for gum, market demand is given by: \[ Q_D = 30 - 5P_D \] and market supply is given by: \[ Q_S = 10 + 5P_S \]
Assume the first 10 units have zero marginal cost. Calculate the market equilibrium price and quantity, and then determine both the consumer surplus and producer surplus in equilibrium.
Supply is specified for nonnegative prices. Assume the first 10 units have zero marginal cost; beyond 10 units, MC = (Q − 10)/5. Thus total variable cost is nonnegative and surplus includes a rectangle plus a triangle. Extending the line to negative prices would be a different model and give different surplus.

Surplus · Solution

Solution: Market Surplus

Step 1: Equilibrium \( P^* \) and \( Q^* \)
Set \( Q_D = Q_S \implies 30 - 5P = 10 + 5P \implies 20 = 10P \implies P^* = \$2 \).
Plugging back in: \( Q^* = 30 - 5(2) = 20 \) units.
Step 2: Consumer Surplus
Choke price is \( P_{\text{choke}} = \$6 \). \[ CS = \frac{1}{2} \times Q^* \times (P_{\text{choke}} - P^*) = \frac{1}{2} \times 20 \times (6 - 2) = \$40 \]
Step 3: Producer Surplus
Supply curve starts at \( Q = 10 \) at \( P = 0 \). The area below \( P^* = 2 \) is a trapezoid: \[ PS = \frac{10 + 20}{2} \times 2 = 15 \times 2 = \$30 \]
Step 4: Total Social Welfare
\[ W = CS + PS = 40 + 30 = \$70 \]

Welfare · Definition

Defining Social Welfare

Definition
Social welfare (or social surplus) is the sum of consumer surplus and producer surplus in the market: \[ W = CS + PS \]
  • Without externalities or other market failures, competitive equilibrium maximizes total surplus. This measures gains from trade; it does not establish fairness.
  • Monopolist pricing reduces social surplus.
Definition: Efficiency
A market is said to be efficient if there is no deadweight loss (or, when social surplus is maximized).

Welfare · Deadweight Loss

Welfare Loss from Restricted Output

In this monopoly example, output is below the efficient quantity:

  • Quantities transacted decrease.
  • Some consumer and producer surplus is lost.
  • The area C represents lost surplus that is not transferred to anyone.

Welfare · Deadweight Loss

Deadweight Loss

Definition
Deadweight loss is the gap between the maximum feasible total surplus and actual total surplus, using the same demand, technology, and resource costs.

Underproduction misses units whose social marginal benefit exceeds social marginal cost. Overproduction adds units whose social cost exceeds their benefit.

A real cost increase can reduce surplus even when the new allocation is efficient.

Benchmark discipline: compare outcomes under the same resource constraints. A transfer changes who receives surplus; deadweight loss reduces the total.

Welfare · Market Power

Welfare Under Market Power

Recall Monopoly Pricing:

  • Monopolists set price above MC, restricting output to maximize profits.
  • This pricing behavior causes a transfer of surplus from consumers to the firm.

Welfare · Market Power

Welfare Under Market Power

The same graph, now with marginal revenue: the monopolist produces where \( MR = MC \), then charges what demand will bear. Press →.

Monopolist’s rule
Produce where \( MR = MC \) at \( Q_m \), then read the price \( P_m \) up on the demand curve — output is restricted below the competitive \( Q_c \).
Surplus areas
CS (competition) \(= A + B + C\)
PS (competition) \(= 0\)
CS (market power) \(= A\)
PS (market power) \(= B\)
Deadweight loss from market power \(= C\)
C is the loss in social surplus that is not transferred to anyone.

Welfare · Practice

Practice: DWL from Market Power

Problem Statement
Consider the iPad market in the figure. Under competition, \( P_c = \$200 \) (equal to MC) and \( Q_c = 160 \)M. With full market power, the monopolist sets \( P_m = \$600 \) and \( Q_m = 80 \)M.

What is the deadweight loss that results from full market power (i.e., monopolist pricing)?

Welfare · Solution

Solution: DWL from Market Power

Step 1: Identify the DWL Triangle (Area C)
The lost transactions run from \( Q_m = 80 \)M to \( Q_c = 160 \)M; the wedge at \( Q_m \) is the gap between the monopoly price and marginal cost: \( P_m - P_c = 600 - 200 = \$400 \).
Step 2: Calculate the Triangle
\[ DWL = \frac{1}{2} \times (Q_c - Q_m) \times (P_m - P_c) = \frac{1}{2} \times 80\text{M} \times 400 = \$16{,}000\text{M} \]

DWL = $16 billion — surplus lost to society, transferred to no one.

Welfare · Deadweight Loss

A Transfer or a Deadweight Loss?

A buyer pays $2 more and a seller receives $2 more, with quantity and real costs unchanged.

Question
Has total surplus fallen? What if a mutually beneficial trade no longer takes place?
The $2 is a transfer: buyer surplus falls and seller surplus rises equally. Forgone trades with benefit above cost create deadweight loss. Someone being worse off is not enough to prove inefficiency.

Welfare · Deadweight Loss

The Inefficiencies of Christmas

Gift giving and value
Waldfogel’s 1993 study asked students to value gifts excluding sentimental value. It compared those valuations with what the gifts cost.

Could a gift be worth less than its purchase price yet still improve well-being? Consider sentimental value and the giver’s enjoyment.

The valuation gap is not a complete welfare measure if it omits sentimental or giver benefits. Source: Joel Waldfogel, “The Deadweight Loss of Christmas,” American Economic Review 83(5), 1993, pp. 1328–1336. Original paper.

Welfare · Shocks

Practice: Welfare Effects of a Shock

Problem Statement
A hypothetical competitive market for identical USB chargers is in equilibrium. Now, supply chain issues raise the marginal cost of production at each quantity.

Perform a welfare analysis, graphically, that shows the impact of this shock. Assume market demand remains unchanged.

Welfare · Solution

Solution: Welfare Effects of a Shock

Step 1: Shift the Supply Curve
Higher marginal production costs shift the supply curve up/left (each quantity now requires a higher price to be supplied). Demand stays put.
Step 2: New Equilibrium
The equilibrium price rises and the equilibrium quantity falls.
Step 3: Welfare Comparison
Consumer surplus shrinks (higher price, fewer units), and social surplus falls relative to the pre-shock market. The new competitive equilibrium is still efficient given the higher costs. Producer surplus can rise or fall; its direction requires more information.
Ask: “Has the economy become poorer, less efficient, or both?” Answer: attainable total surplus falls, but competitive trade remains efficient at the new costs. Producer surplus need not fall; with an initially fixed quantity and higher price it can rise. Do not infer every group loses from aggregate surplus falling.
