A Recap on Welfare

Welfare · Recap

Consumer Surplus + Producer Surplus

The Class 5 picture, in one build. Press →.

Consumer surplus — what buyers were willing to pay, minus what they actually paid.
Producer surplus — what sellers received, minus variable cost.
Social welfare
\[ W = CS + PS \] Without externalities or other market failures, competitive equilibrium maximizes total surplus. Every trade worth making gets made, and no trade that isn’t worth making does.

A market is efficient when there is no deadweight loss.

Welfare · Recap

What Market Power Costs Us

Same market, one seller instead of many. Press →.

One seller faces the whole demand curve, so selling one more unit drags the price down on every unit. That makes \( MR \) fall away below \( D \).
It stops where \( MR = MC \) — a smaller quantity, sold at the much higher price the demand curve will bear. \( P_m > MC \).
Deadweight loss. Between \( Q_m \) and \( Q^* \) sit units buyers value above what they cost to make. Nobody makes them. That surplus is simply gone.

Here, market power restricts beneficial trades, creating this triangle. Hold on to that — we come back to it at the very end of class.

Externalities · A Second Source of DWL

Factory Smoke: A Negative Externality

In the main deck, public goods left surplus on the table by making too little of something good. Externalities are the second way — a producer can ignore harm imposed on other people. Press →.

Externality
A cost (or benefit) landing on third parties, not in any price.
Negative externality
The polluter doesn’t pay the smoke cost ⇒ the market over-produces.
Positive externality
A university or company lab captures only a slice of what its discovery is worth — everyone after it builds on the finding for free ⇒ the market under-produces.
The quantity is the factory’s output, not naturally occurring wildfire smoke. Output creates emissions. Private marginal cost is positive; the source ignores the external damage in this simplified model. Pollution and public-good provision can describe related aspects of the same problem.

Externalities · Back to Welfare

The Deadweight Loss of Pollution

Start from the market’s own \( CS + PS \), then add the smoke society actually pays for. Press →.

Market. \( D = MPC \Rightarrow Q_{\text{market}} \) at price \( P_{\text{mkt}} \).
Consumer surplus — buyers’ gain above the price. The buyers here are whoever wants what the plant makes — the power, the steel, the paper.
Producer surplus — sellers’ gain above cost. The sellers are the plant and its owners.
True cost. Smoke lifts society’s cost: \( MSC = MPC + \text{marginal external cost} \Rightarrow Q^* < Q_{\text{market}} \).
Subtract the damage. The red-hatched band \( (MPC \!\to\! MSC) \) is smoke society bears — take it out of \( CS + PS \).
Deadweight loss. Past \( Q^* \) the damage pokes above demand — those units cost more than they’re worth. That solid red wedge is the DWL.

Externalities · Positive

Positive Externality: Foundational Research

Start from the market’s own \( CS + PS \), then add the knowledge everyone else builds on for free. Press →.

Market. Research gets made up to where funders’ expected private benefit meets the cost of producing it: \( MPB = MC \Rightarrow Q_{\text{market}} \).
Consumer surplus — buyers’ gain above the price. The buyers are those funding the research for private use.
Producer surplus — sellers’ gain above cost. The producers are the universities, private researchers, labs, and everyone else doing the work.
True benefit. Other researchers or firms may benefit without paying: \( MSB = MPB + \text{marginal external benefit} \Rightarrow Q^* > Q_{\text{market}} \).
Add the bonus. The green-hatched band \( (MPB \!\to\! MSB) \) is value society gets from every study — add it to \( CS + PS \).
Deadweight loss. Right of \( Q_{\text{market}} \), MSB still beats MC — that research would add value but is never funded. The red wedge is that loss.

Externalities · The Rule

Private and Social Values

MPC and MPB are marginal private cost and benefit. MSC and MSB include effects on third parties.

Polluting production
MSC = MPC + marginal external harm. Private output is too high when buyers receive all benefits.
Beneficial spillovers
MSB = MPB + marginal external benefit. Private output is too low when sellers bear all costs.

Efficiency requires MSB = MSC at the best interior quantity.

Externalities · The Fix

Corrective Taxes and Subsidies

Negative production externality
A per-unit tax equal to marginal external harm at the efficient quantity can make the producer face the right incentive.
Positive external benefit
A per-unit subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit at the efficient quantity can encourage more of the activity.

The policy amount uses the external effect of one more unit, not total external damage or benefit.

For constant marginal external harm, the tax shifts MPC up to MSC everywhere. With varying harm, a constant tax need only match marginal harm at the optimum. Assume competition and no other distortions. Government must estimate these effects; a poorly chosen rate need not improve welfare.

Why It Matters

Evaluate the Policy

  • Identify the market failure: which costs or benefits are missing from private choices?
  • Explain how the policy changes incentives.
  • Compare gains with implementation costs and unintended effects.
  • Describe who pays and who benefits.

Public funding, pollution taxes, and subsidies can improve outcomes when well targeted.

Synthesis

It All Comes Back to Welfare

One yardstick has run through Classes 5–6 — total surplus (welfare):

Setting Quantity traded Welfare
Perfect competition Just right — \( Q = Q^* \) Maximized — no DWL
Monopoly / market power Too little — \( Q < Q^* \) Deadweight loss \( (P > MC) \)
Taxes & price controls Too little — \( Q < Q^* \) Deadweight loss
Public goods Too little — often none at all Deadweight loss
Negative externalities Too much — \( Q > Q^* \) Deadweight loss
Positive externalities Too little — \( Q < Q^* \) Deadweight loss

Every row after the first is the same failure: the quantity traded isn’t \( Q^* \), so surplus that could have existed simply doesn’t. In these linear examples, the avoidable surplus loss is a triangle — it sits to the left of \( Q^* \) when we make too little, and to the right when we make too much.

Efficiency and distribution
Assess total surplus and distribution separately. A policy must be compared with a feasible alternative under the same conditions.
The table summarizes the stated models: competitive markets without externalities; monopoly with an interior solution; quantity-reducing taxes and binding controls in otherwise efficient markets; underfunded public goods; and the illustrated externalities. Nonbinding controls, perfectly inelastic supply or demand, and corrective taxes are exceptions. A loss from higher real resource costs is not necessarily inefficiency.

Quick Check

🎲 Which Way Does It Miss?

For each: positive or negative externality? Does the market make too much or too little? Which curve moves — \( MSC \) or \( MSB \)?

  • A factory dumping waste into a river
  • Your neighbour planting a beautiful garden
  • Getting a college education
  • Driving into downtown at rush hour
—
Answers
Waste = negative, \( MSC > MPC \), over-produced. Garden = positive, \( MSB > MPB \), under-produced. College = positive, \( MSB > MPB \), under-produced. Rush-hour driving = negative (congestion), \( MSC > MPC \), over-produced.

Rule of thumb: in these examples, the relevant social curve sits above the private one — cost side for negatives, benefit side for positives.

The curve direction is specific to the examples, not all externalities. Positive production spillovers can make MSC lower than MPC; negative consumption externalities can make MSB lower than MPB. For the garden and education examples, focus on benefits to others beyond private enjoyment or earnings.

The Game · The Street

Same Street, Same Gas, Two Signs

Two stations, fifty metres apart, on the same side of the road, selling the identical product. Every driver can read both boards before deciding where to pull in. Watch the prices.

The Game · Setup

Two Stations, One Corner

The market
Inverse demand for gas on this corner is \( P = 5 - 0.5Q \) — equivalently, \( Q = 10 - 2P \). It costs each station \( MC = \$1 \) per unit, and there are no fixed costs.
The rules (each station can serve the whole market)
  • You and your partner each secretly pick a price, at the same time.
  • The lower price gets every single customer.
  • A tie splits the market 50/50.
  • The higher price sells nothing at all.

Pair up. Write your price on a scrap of paper — don’t let your rival see it. Any price you like, to the cent: $0.00 to $5.00.

The Game · Debrief

So What Just Happened?

Three questions for the room
  1. If you both had held at $3.00, you’d each have banked $4.00. Did either player want to undercut? Why?
  2. Where did our prices end up after five rounds — and where were they heading?
  3. Only two firms are on this corner. Does it feel like a monopoly? Where would you put this market on the sliding scale we saw just before playing?
The incentive to undercut
You couldn’t hold at $3.00 because shading a single cent underneath doubled your money — $4.00 for matching, nearly $8.00 for undercutting. And your rival was doing exactly the same arithmetic about you.

That reasoning — “what should I do, given what I expect them to do?” — is the whole subject we start now. It has a name: game theory.

Part 5

What Makes a Game?

You just played one. Now let’s name its parts. Press ↓

What Makes a Game · Players & Actions (1/4)

Players and Actions

Definition: Players
All games need players. Players are economic agents — e.g. friends, consumers, firms, the government, or even Nature (yes, the weather can be a player).
Definition: Actions
Every player can play some number of actions — e.g. “move left”, “pay a fine”, “don’t enter a market”, or “post $2.95 on the sign”.

Each player’s actions come with rewards (positive payoff) or “punishment” (negative payoff).

In our game
Players: Station A and Station B.   Actions: every price from $0.00 to $5.00.   Payoffs: profit in dollars.

What Makes a Game · Strategies (2/4)

Actions and Strategies

An action is a choice made at a decision point. A strategy is a complete plan for every decision point a player might reach.

In a one-shot simultaneous pricing game, a pure strategy is simply one price. You cannot condition today’s price on a rival’s price you have not observed.

In a repeated game, a strategy can depend on prices observed in earlier rounds.

Ask: “Post $2.99 today” is a one-shot pure strategy. “Match yesterday’s rival price” is a repeated-game plan. Beliefs about a rival inform the choice; they are not observable branches in a one-shot strategy.

What Makes a Game · The Normal Form (3/4)

Payoffs and the Normal Form

Definition: Payoff & Normal Form
A payoff is the outcome of a game, and it depends on the strategies every player selects. We write players, strategies, and payoffs into a grid called the normal form.

Our corner has too many prices to draw, so shrink it to two: hold at $3, or cut to $2. (Profits straight off the table you just used.)

What Makes a Game · Rules (4/4)

Simultaneous-Move, One-Shot Games

Players, actions, strategies, payoffs give the basic structure of a game. We still need rules.

Our rules, named
You and your partner picked prices at the same time, neither able to see the other — and in any single round, you played once. That is a simultaneous-move, one-shot game.
Definitions
In a simultaneous-move game, players choose without observing the others’ current choices. A one-shot game is one they play exactly once.

The big question: in a game like this, what does an equilibrium even look like?

The class activity has five rounds, so it is repeated interaction. The payoff matrix analyzes one round in isolation. A one-shot equilibrium alone does not prove how participants will play or that prices must converge in five rounds.

Equilibrium Concepts · Dominance (1/5)

Dominant and Dominated Strategies

Strictly dominant
A strategy yields a higher payoff than every other strategy for every possible choice by the other players.
Strictly dominated
Another single strategy yields a higher payoff for every possible choice by the other players.

Compare the same alternatives across every row or column. A strategy is not dominated merely because it loses in one case.

We use strict dominance here. Weak dominance allows ties everywhere and requires a strict improvement somewhere. A never-best response is not the definition of a strictly dominated strategy without further qualifications.

Equilibrium Concepts · DSE (2/5)

Dominant Strategy Equilibrium

Definition: Dominant Strategy Equilibrium (DSE)
A dominant strategy equilibrium (DSE) is an equilibrium where every player plays their dominant strategy (if one exists for every player).

In the restricted $3-or-$2 game, the DSE is (Cut to $2, Cut to $2), paying ($3, $3).

A warning about the word “dominant”
Dominant does not mean good. Both stations playing their dominant strategy earn $3 each — less than the $4 each they’d get from both holding at $3. Rational play made them both worse off.

Equilibrium Concepts · Nash Equilibrium (3–4/5)

Best-Responses & Nash Equilibrium

Dominance is demanding — most games have no dominant strategy at all. So zoom out: ask for the best you can do given what everyone else is doing.

Definition: Best-Response
A player’s best-response is the strategy giving them the highest payoff — taking all other players’ strategies as given.
Definition: Nash Equilibrium (NE)
A Nash equilibrium is an outcome where every player is playing a best-response — they are mutual best-responses.

Notice a dominant strategy is a best-response to everything — so every DSE is automatically a Nash equilibrium. Not the other way round.

And we have already done the work: those four checks at the start of this part were exactly the four best-responses. The cell that collected both underlines — (Cut, Cut) = ($3, $3) — is our Nash equilibrium.

Equilibrium Concepts · Prisoner’s Dilemma (5/5)

The Prisoner’s Dilemma

In this two-action game, each station has a strictly dominant action: cut to $2.

Both cutting gives ($3, $3). Both holding at $3 gives ($4, $4), which both stations prefer, but each wants to undercut when the other holds.

Question
Does a Nash equilibrium have to be the outcome the players jointly prefer?
No. In this prisoner’s dilemma, dominant actions lead to an equilibrium that both players rank below mutual cooperation. Not every game with an inefficient Nash equilibrium is a prisoner’s dilemma. Gains to drivers should be counted separately from firms’ profits.

Back to the Corner · The Unravelling

It Doesn’t Stop at $2

Our 2×2 grid only let you choose $3 or $2. On the real corner you had every cent in between. Press →.

With continuously adjustable prices, the Nash equilibrium is: \( P = MC = \$1 \), profit $0 each
Check it. Raise your price above $1 and you sell nothing. Drop it below $1 and every gallon loses money. Match at $1 and you earn zero. Raising price also earns zero; selling below cost loses money. Neither of you can do better on your own. With prices in whole cents, ($1.01, $1.01) is also a Nash equilibrium: matching earns $0.0399 each; cutting to $1 earns $0.
In the exact 501-price game played in class, the two pure Nash equilibria are (1.00, 1.00) and (1.01, 1.01). Check every unilateral deviation. At 1.01, each sells 3.99 units and earns 0.0399; undercutting to 1 yields zero. The continuous-price Bertrand model has the unique pure equilibrium at marginal cost. Do not equate iterative undercutting with a proof of convergence.

The Bertrand paradox
With identical products, equal constant MC, enough capacity, and continuously adjustable prices, two price-setting firms deliver P = MC. The whole-cent game can stop one cent above MC. Go back and drag that marker to the far left — a duopoly landed at the competitive end of the scale.

Which is why real gas stations work so hard not to be identical — loyalty cards, car washes, better coffee, a location one exit closer. Every one of those is an attempt to escape this corner.

The table’s zero-profit and no-DWL benchmark describes the continuous-price model. At the cent-grid equilibrium (1.01, 1.01), each earns 0.0399 and DWL = 0.0001. Product differentiation, capacity limits, unequal costs, and repeated interaction can change predictions. Ask students which assumption a real gas station violates.
