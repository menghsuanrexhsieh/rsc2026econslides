Interventions · Rationale

Why Intervene in Markets?

The competitive benchmark assumes that prices reflect all relevant costs and benefits and that buyers and sellers can make informed choices.

  • Externalities: a transaction affects people outside it.
  • Public goods: providers may struggle to collect payment from beneficiaries.
  • Distribution: people may prefer a different division of income or access, even at an efficiency cost.

Evaluate a policy’s benefits, costs, and effects on different groups.

Interventions · Price Controls

Price Ceilings & Price Floors

Definition
Price controls set a legal maximum or minimum price. A nonbinding control leaves the competitive equilibrium unchanged.
  • Price Ceiling (\( P_c \)): Legal maximum price. Binding only if below equilibrium (\( P_c < P^* \)), causing a shortage.
  • Price Floor (\( P_f \)): Legal minimum price. Binding only if above equilibrium (\( P_f > P^* \)), causing a surplus.

In this model, with no government purchases, quantity traded is the smaller of Qs and Qd.

The basic diagram assumes that willing buyers and sellers can transact. Queues, search, evasion, or inefficient rationing can further change welfare. A floor with government purchases has a different quantity outcome.

Interventions · Taxes

A Per-Unit Tax

How do taxes affect market outcomes?

  • A per-unit tax is a fixed charge on each unit traded.
  • A tax creates a wedge between what consumers pay (\( P_b \)) and what sellers receive (\( P_s \)).
  • In an otherwise efficient market, a tax creates DWL if it reduces quantity traded.
Tax Identity
\[ P_b = P_s + t \] where \( t \) is the per-unit tax rate.

Interventions · Taxes

Anatomy of a Tax: Wedge, Revenue & DWL

A market at equilibrium \( (Q^* = 40,\ P^* = \$3) \) meets a big \( \$3 \) per-unit tax — drawn large (not to scale) so every piece shows. Press → to add each piece.

Tax wedge \( t = P_b - P_s \) ⇒ quantity falls ⇒ the trades between \( Q_{\text{tax}} \) and \( Q^* \) vanish. That lost surplus is the DWL triangle.

Interventions · Taxes

Tax Revenue and Deadweight Loss

Tax revenue is a transfer to government. Add it when calculating surplus after a tax:

\[ W=CS+PS+\text{government revenue}. \]

Deadweight loss measures gains from trades that the tax prevents. In this model, a tax that leaves quantity unchanged creates no such loss.

Check
Does a $1 increase in tax revenue necessarily mean a $1 increase in DWL?
No. Revenue is a transfer; DWL measures lost total surplus. A truly choice-independent lump-sum tax does not create a substitution distortion, though it can affect income and distribution. A corrective tax can improve welfare when an externality exists.

Interventions · Taxes

Taxes Can Affect When People Marry

In the U.S., married couples generally choose between joint and separate filing. Marriage can raise or lower combined taxes depending on incomes and tax rules.

Filing status generally depends on marital status on the last day of the tax year.

Hypothetical choice
If marrying in December would raise this year’s combined tax bill, how might that affect the incentive to marry in December versus January?
Holding other considerations fixed, the couple has a tax incentive to delay until January. This is a prediction, not proof of a behavioral effect. The original citation to Gelardi concerned Canada and England/Wales, not U.S. evidence. Current filing-status source: IRS filing status. Exact tax effects depend on the applicable year and household circumstances.

When Market Outcomes Are Inefficient

Without market failures, competitive trade maximizes total surplus in our model.

With external costs or benefits, or difficulty collecting payment for public goods, private choices can leave gains unrealized.

A policy can improve the outcome if its benefits exceed its implementation costs and other harms.

The Core Problem

The Free-Rider Problem

  • Cleaner outdoor air benefits many residents.
  • A resident may benefit whether or not they contribute to its cost.
  • Voluntary contributions can therefore fall short of the cost of improvements whose total benefits exceed their costs.
Question
Two residents each value a shared improvement at $40. It costs $60. Is it socially worthwhile? Would either resident fund it alone?
Total benefit is $80, exceeding the $60 cost. Neither would pay the whole $60 alone if motivated only by their own $40 benefit. Splitting the cost makes both better off, but collecting contributions can be difficult. Private provision is possible through donations, sponsorship, or cooperation; underprovision is not necessarily zero provision.

Part 2 · Takeaway

Public Goods and Collective Action

A public good is non-rival and non-excludable. Those properties can make voluntary funding too low.

Governments can finance provision through taxes or coordinate pollution reduction. Communities, charities, and firms can also contribute.

Compare the total benefits of more provision with its resource and administrative costs. Government provision is not automatically efficient.

Summary

Efficiency and Distribution

  • Without market failures, competitive equilibrium maximizes gains from trade in our model.
  • Taxes and binding controls can prevent beneficial trades; tax revenue itself is a transfer.
  • Public goods and externalities can justify collective action.
  • Efficiency and fairness are different questions. Ask who gains, who loses, and how total surplus changes.
